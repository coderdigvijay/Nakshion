# Eclipses and Special Days

This file describes **traditional observances in neutral, descriptive terms**. It is cultural and religious information. It is not medical advice: for health-related fasting (pregnancy, diabetes, medication, age, illness), a doctor's guidance takes precedence, and most traditions themselves relax fasting rules for children, the elderly, pregnant women and the unwell.

## Eclipses (Grahana)

An eclipse is understood astronomically as the Sun, Earth and Moon aligning near the lunar nodes. A **solar eclipse (Surya Grahana)** occurs at the new moon (Amavasya) when the Moon passes in front of the Sun; a **lunar eclipse (Chandra Grahana)** at the full moon (Purnima) when the Moon enters Earth's shadow. In Vedic mythology the nodes **Rahu and Ketu** are said to "swallow" the luminaries, which is the origin of the word *graha* ("seizer") and of the node-based calculation: an eclipse occurs when the Sun-Moon conjunction or opposition happens close to a lunar node (roughly within 10 to 18 degrees depending on the body and eclipse type; exact limits are computed by ephemeris).

**Traditional framing:** the classical view treats the eclipse period as a time of heightened spiritual potency. Practices often described:

- **Sutak (Sutaka):** a period of restraint before the eclipse. The commonly published rule is **12 hours (4 prahars) before a solar eclipse and 9 hours (3 prahars) before a lunar eclipse**, running until the eclipse ends. Common restraints: avoiding new ceremonies and sometimes cooking and eating; temples often close their sanctums. Many sources apply the rule **only where the eclipse is visible** and state that a penumbral lunar eclipse is generally not counted for Sutak [medium confidence; practice varies]. Children, elderly and the unwell are typically allowed shorter restraint (one prahar, about 3 hours).
- **During the eclipse:** quiet mantra repetition (japa), prayer, meditation, reading of sacred texts.
- **After the eclipse (moksha):** a ritual bath, fresh clothes, cleaning the house and temple, and **charity (dana)**; the Indian Calendar of Sewell and Dikshit notes that royal gifts were often timed to an eclipse because charitable acts then were considered especially meritorious.
- **Kurukshetra and river-bank bathing fairs** during solar eclipses are a documented pilgrimage tradition.
- Some families store food with **tulsi leaves or kusha grass** during the eclipse; this is a customary practice, not a scientific claim.

**Muhurta implication:** new auspicious beginnings are traditionally deferred during the eclipse and often for a short time around it; the Moon and Sun's sign and nakshatra at the eclipse are also read by some astrologers for mundane themes (see `mundane_astrology.md`). A natal chart's sensitive points close to an eclipse degree are treated by some astrologers as "activated"; present that as a tradition-specific interpretive idea, not a prediction.

**Modern safety note (not a tradition):** looking directly at a solar eclipse without proper solar-viewing filters can damage the eyes. This is true regardless of any astrological framing.

## Ekadashi (the 11th tithi)

Ekadashi occurs twice a month (Shukla and Krishna), 24 in a normal year with 26 in a year with Adhika masa. Each Ekadashi has its own name and story (for example **Nirjala/Bhimaseni** in Jyeshtha, **Devshayani/Shayani** in Ashadha, **Prabodhini/Devutthani** in Kartika, **Vaikuntha** in Margashirsha/Pausha). Traditionally Ekadashi is devoted to **Vishnu**. The observance is a **fast or restricted diet** (no grains and pulses in many traditions; fruit, milk, tubers allowed in many households), prayer, scripture reading and charity, with the fast broken (**parana**) the next morning after sunrise within the Dwadashi window. Because the tithi can overlap two sunrises, Smarta and Vaishnava traditions sometimes observe on adjacent days. Fasting is voluntary and adjustable to health.

## Sankashti Chaturthi (the Krishna Chaturthi)

**Sankashti Chaturthi** is the fourth tithi of the Krishna (waning) fortnight, devoted to **Ganesha**. Devotees traditionally fast through the day and break the fast at night after sighting the moon and offering prayers to Ganesha. The Sankashti that falls on a **Tuesday** is called **Angaraki Chaturthi** and is regarded as especially significant. The Shukla Chaturthi is the **Vinayaka (Ganesha) Chaturthi**, the lunar-fortnight counterpart; the annual festival **Ganesh Chaturthi** is the Bhadrapada Shukla Chaturthi. Moonrise times on Sankashti are place-specific, which is why almanacs list the observance time by city.

## Pradosha

**Pradosha** is the twilight (about 1.5 hours around sunset) on the **Trayodashi (13th tithi)** of each fortnight, dedicated to **Shiva**. Devotees visit Shiva temples and may fast during the day. When it falls on Monday it is called **Soma Pradosha**; on Saturday, **Shani Pradosha**. Observance depends on Trayodashi being present during the evening twilight (not just at sunrise), which is a rule almanacs apply when they list the date.

## Amavasya (new moon)

**Amavasya** is the tithi when the Sun and Moon share the same longitude; the Moon is not visible. Traditions attached to it:

- **Ancestor remembrance (pitru tarpana, shraddha):** water and sesame offerings; most prominent on **Mahalaya Amavasya** at the end of **Pitru Paksha** (the dark fortnight of Bhadrapada/Ashvina), when families honour ancestors.
- **Somvati Amavasya** (Monday), **Shani Amavasya** (Saturday) and **Mauni Amavasya** (Magha) are named instances with specific observances such as silence, river bathing, charity, worship of Shani or Shiva.
- **Diwali** is celebrated on the Amavasya of Ashvina (amanta) / Kartika (purnimanta) with lamps symbolising light over darkness.
- Muhurta practice generally defers new ventures and auspicious ceremonies on Amavasya; many sources treat it as an excellent day for reflection, cleaning up, and completions.

## Purnima (full moon)

**Purnima** is the tithi when the Moon is opposite the Sun and fully lit. Widely considered auspicious: a common day for **Satyanarayana Puja**, charity, pilgrimage bathing and fasting. Many named full moons: **Guru Purnima** (Ashadha; honours teachers), **Raksha Bandhan** (Shravana), **Sharad Purnima** (Ashvina), **Kartika Purnima / Dev Diwali**, **Buddha Purnima** (Vaishakha), **Holi** (Phalguna), **Magha Purnima**. Lunar eclipses occur only on Purnima. Some people describe fuller emotions and poorer sleep around the full moon; scientific support for this is mixed and astrology presents it only as a reflective theme.

## Other regular observances (brief)

- **Masik Shivaratri:** Krishna Chaturdashi each month; Maha Shivaratri is the annual one.
- **Skanda Shashthi** (Shukla Shashthi) for Kartikeya in some traditions.
- **Vinayaka Chaturthi** (Shukla Chaturthi) for Ganesha.
- **Navaratri** (the first nine tithis of Shukla paksha in Chaitra and Ashvina) devoted to the goddess; Ashvina's is the major one.

## Giving the user a calendar answer

State the **observance, the tithi it is tied to, and the date for their city and the almanac convention**, and mention if different traditions might choose adjacent days. Keep the tone descriptive ("traditionally observed by...") and respect that the person may follow a different tradition or none.
